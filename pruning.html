Pruning Guide

The rules of thumb · read before the first cut of the season

✂ The Basics
  1. Start with the three D's. Any time of year, remove wood that is dead, damaged or diseased. Then take out branches that cross and rub.
  2. Prune spring bloomers after they flower. Lilac, forsythia, mock orange and old roses set next year's buds on this year's wood. Cutting in winter cuts off the flowers.
  3. Prune summer bloomers in late winter or early spring. Butterfly bush, panicle hydrangea, roses that repeat, and most fruit trees bloom on new wood. Cut hard while dormant; they grow back and flower the same year.
  4. Never take more than a third. Removing more in one year shocks the plant and brings a thicket of water sprouts.
  5. Cut just above an outward-facing bud or a branch collar, sloping slightly away from the bud. No stubs, no flush cuts.
  6. Sharp, clean tools. Wipe blades with rubbing alcohol between plants — especially after cutting anything that looked sick.
  7. Don't prune in autumn. Fresh cuts and soft new growth going into frost is how you lose a shrub. Wait for dormancy.
✂ The Methods

Pinch

Nip out the soft growing tip with your fingers. The plant branches below the pinch and stays bushy.

Basil, chrysanthemum, dahlia, coleus, young annuals.

Deadhead

Snip off spent flowers just above the next leaf or bud. Stops seed-setting so the plant keeps blooming.

Roses, butterfly bush, geraniums, salvia, most perennials.

Thin

Remove whole stems right back to the base or a main branch. Opens the plant to light and air without changing its shape.

Lilac, spirea, forsythia, overcrowded shrubs and fruit trees.

Cut Back

Shorten stems to a set height, usually to a bud or framework. A "hard" cut back goes down to 6–18 in.

Butterfly bush, perennials in spring, lavender (lightly), hybrid tea roses.

Shear

Trim the whole surface evenly with hedge shears. Keeps a formal shape; do it a little and often.

Hedges, boxwood, yew, lavender after bloom.

Limb Up

Remove the lowest branches at the trunk to raise the canopy. Cut outside the branch collar, three-cut method on anything heavy.

Trees over paths and lawns, crab apple, magnolia, mature shrubs turned into small trees.

✂ Pruning Calendar
Late Winter
  • Hard-prune summer-flowering shrubs: butterfly bush, panicle and smooth hydrangea, repeat roses.
  • Shape apple and pear trees while the branches are bare and easy to read.
  • Cut ornamental grasses to a few inches before new blades push up.
Spring
  • Cut back last year's perennial stems once new growth shows at the base.
  • Trim lavender and rosemary lightly into green wood only, never bare stems.
  • After lilac, forsythia and quince finish flowering, thin out a few of the oldest stems.
Summer
  • Deadhead roses, butterfly bush and perennials every week or two to keep them blooming.
  • Pinch basil, dahlias and mums until midsummer for bushier plants.
  • Prune stone fruit (cherry, plum) in dry weather to avoid silver leaf disease.
  • Light shear of hedges after the first flush of growth.
Autumn
  • Mostly leave the secateurs in the shed. Remove only broken or diseased wood.
  • Cut back anything that flops onto paths; leave seed heads for the birds.
  • Clean, sharpen and oil the tools before storing them.
Tools
  • Bypass pruners for live stems up to ½ in. Two blades pass like scissors — a clean cut that heals.
  • Loppers for stems ½–1½ in. Long handles give the leverage.
  • Pruning saw for anything thicker. Cut from below first on heavy limbs so the bark doesn't tear.
  • Hedge shears for shaping only, never for thinning.
  • Rubbing alcohol and a rag to wipe blades between plants.
Where to Cut

On a stem: about ¼ in. above a bud that points the way you want the new shoot to grow. Angle the cut so water runs off away from the bud.

On a branch: just outside the collar, the slight swelling where branch meets trunk. Leave the collar; it seals the wound.

Heavy limbs: three cuts. An undercut a foot out, a top cut a little further out to drop the weight, then the final cut at the collar.
